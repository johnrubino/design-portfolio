Designing for a System in Transition

BNY Pershing was consolidating two platforms into one. I helped reimagine how advisors open accounts and move assets — two workflows that touch millions of dollars daily but were failing users at every turn.

Dimension Details
My Role Product Designer — I owned the New Account Opening design end-to-end and designed the collapsing stepper pattern for Asset Movement
Team 2 Studio Designers, Design Lead, Product Partners, User Research Team
Timeline ~8 months
Outcome Validated, development-ready designs for both workflows — documented and staged for implementation as the platform consolidation roadmap advances

Two platforms, one future

BNY Pershing's Wealth Services group operates two platforms: NetX360+, a legacy system used by traditional broker-dealers and their advisors, and Wove, a newer platform built for RIAs. The company's long-term strategy is to consolidate these into a single unified experience — Wove — with features white-labeled based on client needs.

But you can't flip a switch. NetX360 has decades of features Wove doesn't yet support. The transition happens piece by piece, workflow by workflow.

Our team was asked to envision what two critical workflows — New Account Opening and Asset Movement — should look like in that future state. Not incremental improvements to the legacy system, but a rethinking of how these experiences should work when built from the ground up.

🔀

Platform context diagram — NetX360 and Wove as two separate systems with an arrow pointing toward a unified future platform

One project, two workflows

This case study covers both workflows in sequence. They share a research foundation and a common design philosophy, but each had distinct problems and required distinct solutions.

Part One covers New Account Opening — a multi-participant workflow with the highest advisor pain point rating in our research.

Part Two covers Asset Movement — a more transactional flow where the legacy interface was creating directional fear and operational risk.

The design principles that emerged from each, and how they converged, are documented in the closing section.

Part One
New Account Opening
🔍

Discovery: Understanding the Problem Before Designing the Solution

Before any design work began, our research team conducted extensive discovery across two workstreams. The Custody Client Research surveyed clients spanning every key persona — advisors, operations staff, client associates, compliance managers, and senior leaders — to understand where pain was concentrated across the full scope of advisor responsibilities. Running alongside this, the team conducted moderated research sessions and a full journey mapping exercise to understand how account opening actually worked in practice, firm by firm, day by day.

The picture that emerged wasn't abstract. It was specific, consistent, and in some cases striking. Here's what we found.

Account opening was the single biggest pain point advisors had

When asked to rate pain across all their daily tasks, advisors ranked "Onboard clients and/or open accounts" as their highest pain point — above trading, transfers, and every other workflow.

Zooming out from that finding, four themes emerged across all the research that would anchor every design decision that followed:

  1. Intuitiveness over efficiency — Reducing reliance on external help — calls to reps, tech support, help desks — was critical. Intuitiveness directly correlates to speed.
  2. Integration and interoperability — Users wanted data entered once and populated everywhere. They wanted their existing tools to communicate.
  3. Less is more — Users wanted to complete only required fields. Less input meant less time spent and more cognitive bandwidth for actual client work.
  4. Information organization — Users wanted systematic ways to track progress, clearer signals about what was required versus missing, and persistent visibility into where they were in the process.

The process was an ecosystem problem, not a form problem

Digging deeper into how account opening actually worked in the field, the scale of the problem became clearer. Participants reported using at least 10 separate tools as part of their account opening process — Wealthbox, eMoney, Advent APX, Juncture, Envestnet, DST, NetX360, email, cloud storage, and handwritten notes.

The process itself took anywhere from 3 to 30 days depending on account type, compliance requirements, firm-specific processes, and how much back-and-forth was needed to gather client information.

This wasn't a usability problem with a single interface. It was an ecosystem problem with no center of gravity.

And when we looked at the tool they were supposed to be using — NetX360 — it wasn't helping. Account opening presented itself as a clean 5-step process: Getting Started → Enter Account Info → Review Clients & Accounts → Choose Forms & Signature → Submit. The reality behind step two was a monolithic scrolling form — dozens of fields spanning client details, account attributes, regulatory requirements, and custodian features — with no indication of how much remained, which fields were required, or whether something critical had been missed.

Pain clustered at every phase — and worst at the end

The journey mapping work traced the full account opening experience across six phases: Setup → Prepare → Input → Get Signature → Check Status & Resolve Issues → Locate New Account. Pain was present throughout, but it concentrated hardest at "Check Status & Resolve Issues" — where NIGO (Not In Good Order) rejections accumulated and users had no clear path to understanding what was wrong or who could fix it.

The specific failure patterns were consistent across firms, regardless of size or structure.

Configuration didn't carry over. When users copied an existing client's setup, preferences like e-delivery settings wouldn't transfer. Joint account configurations applied to one account but not both.

"My eDelivery preferences, I should be able to do that across the board for everybody or my additional account information — none of this ever flows over. I want it to be the same across everybody across all account types. I can tell you this is very, very time consuming."

— First Command

Systems didn't talk to each other. Advisors ran NetX360 and their CRM in parallel windows, manually syncing status between them after every action.

"Our CRM doesn't talk to NetX360 at all, so the advisors are basically doing it in parallel. They've got NetX360 open, they've got the CRM open, so they have a Pershing account assigned to them, they go upload the documents into NetX360 to kick off this account opening. Then update my workflow and now the account got opened. In the CRM they mark the account got open, account open date."

— Ops Manager, Captrust

Multiple accounts meant multiple signature envelopes. Opening five accounts for one client meant five separate envelopes — a friction point with an obvious fix that had never been made.

"If we have multiple accounts, we'll open multiple accounts. We would like to have instead of five different envelopes go, if it's five different accounts, have one envelope go."

— COO, Davenport

The cognitive picture: hidden complexity at every turn

Beyond the workflow failures, research surfaced a deeper cognitive burden. Different tools used different terminology. Compliance minimums varied by firm. Context lived in a dozen places at once. Advisors weren't just filling out forms — they were holding an entire process in their heads because nothing was designed to hold it for them.

What users consistently described wanting was straightforward: in-context help when they had questions, the ability to toggle optional fields on only when relevant, a clear view of what was pending and who owned it, and a way to collect sensitive client data directly rather than relaying it over the phone.

The finding that shaped everything: advisors never start with complete information

One discovery finding had more design implications than any other: advisors rarely have all the information they need when they begin an application. They start with what they have, then spend days or weeks chasing clients for income details, beneficiary information, and regulatory disclosures.

The legacy system had no model for this reality. You either completed the form or you didn't. There was no partial progress, no way to flag what was missing, and no mechanism for routing specific fields to clients for direct input.

📈

Journey gap analysis — discovery research mapping the gap between how advisors actually worked and what the legacy tool assumed about them.

What the research pointed toward

Taken together, the discovery work made one thing clear: this wasn't about making the form shorter or the interface cleaner. It was about four structural failures that needed structural solutions:

  1. Visibility — Users couldn't see where they were, what was complete, or what was blocking them
  2. Flexibility — A linear form didn't match non-linear work; users needed to move freely across sections
  3. Integration — 10+ tools meant constant context-switching and repeated manual data entry
  4. Communication — Missing information required chasing clients through separate channels with no audit trail

Our design benchmark became the "TurboTax-like experience" that participants kept referencing — guided, intuitive, and honest about complexity rather than burying it.

The Core Question

"How might we make a complex, multi-participant workflow navigable without hiding its true complexity?"

🔭

Exploring the Solution Space

With research findings defined, we moved into structured exploration. The goal wasn't to find the most visually interesting pattern — it was to find the structural model that best fit how advisors actually worked.

Pre-flow: reducing complexity before it starts

One research insight stood out: users were spending significant time configuring accounts that could have been set up faster with the right upfront decisions. We explored a pre-flow pattern that would capture key choices — custodian, account type, sub-type — before entering the main form.

This served two purposes: it filtered out irrelevant fields based on account type, and it gave users a sense of forward momentum before encountering more complex form sections.

Three competing navigation approaches

For the core form, we evaluated three distinct structural patterns:

Approach 1 — Sidebar stepper

A traditional stepped sidebar showing major sections: Client Details, Account Details, Documents, Funding Instructions, Review. Linear, familiar, but limited visibility into sub-tasks within each step.

Approach 2 — To-Do dashboard

A fundamentally different model — presenting the entire workflow as a task list with completion percentages. Sections show status at a glance and expand to reveal remaining items. This reframed NAO as a project to manage rather than a form to fill out.

Approach 3 — Full-page form with progress indicators

Keeping the form as the primary experience but adding progress signals and section navigation. Less structural change, more incremental improvement to the existing paradigm.

The open design questions

Exploration surfaced specific decisions we needed to resolve:

  • Where does the To-Do list or stepper live? Sidebar? Top nav? Collapsible?
  • Where is the "Next" button? Fixed footer? Inline with content? In the nav?
  • How do we handle sub-flows? Modal overlay? Full page? Dedicated flow?
  • What's the right level of detail? Major sections only? Every field group?

These weren't visual preferences — they had direct implications for how users would understand and move through complexity.

🖥️

Wireframe detail — open design question annotations around navigation placement and sub-flow handling. Open questions mapped visually: each decision had downstream implications for how users would navigate complexity.

The collaborative flow

One exploration directly addressed the finding that advisors often lacked complete client information mid-application. We designed a flow where advisors could invite clients to fill in protected personal information (PPI) directly — Social Security numbers, income details, financial information clients might not want to share over the phone.

The flow branched: advisors would collect basic contact information first, then send a secure link to the client. The client would complete their section, the data would merge back into the application, and both parties could see what was complete versus pending.

This concept surfaced a specific UI element: a "Send to client" toggle on form sections containing PPI — a persistent affordance that made the collaborative model explicit rather than buried in settings.

The To-Do List pattern emerges

After evaluating all three approaches against the research findings, the To-Do List pattern was the clear direction. It was the only model that addressed all four structural problems identified in discovery simultaneously:

  • Visibility — every section and its completion status visible at all times
  • Flexibility — users can navigate to any section at any point, in any order
  • Communication — missing or incomplete sections flagged persistently, not buried in error states
  • Progress — completion percentage gives users a genuine sense of forward movement

The key structural insight: instead of choosing between a linear stepper or a task dashboard, we could have both — a persistent navigation that functions as a live checklist, with section-level completion states and free navigation throughout.

With a clear structural direction established, we moved into design.

✨

The Design

The exploration phase gave us a structural model. The design phase gave it form — working through what the To-Do List actually looked like in practice, how each component behaved, and how the collaborative flow translated into a finished UI.

The To-Do List in high fidelity

The final NAO experience opens with the pre-flow — a focused three-step setup that captures custodian, account type, and sub-type before the main form begins. These upfront decisions filter out irrelevant fields downstream and give users a sense of momentum before encountering more complex sections.

🖥️

Pre-flow final design — Custodian → Account Type → Sub Type in high fidelity. The pre-flow in final form: three decisions that scope the experience before it begins.

From there, the To-Do List becomes the persistent backbone of the experience. Every section is visible in the left rail with its completion state — not started, in progress, complete. Users can jump to any section at any time. The form panel on the right updates to reflect wherever they are.

The send-to-client capability

One of the most direct design responses to a research finding: advisors rarely have all client information when they begin an application, and the legacy system had no model for that reality. The finished design surfaces a "Send to client" toggle on any form section containing protected personal information — Social Security numbers, income details, financial data clients might not want to relay verbally.

The flow is straightforward: an advisor completes the fields they have access to, toggles the section to send, and the client receives a secure link to fill in their portion directly. Both parties can see what's complete and what's pending without any back-and-forth.

Validating the Design

With the core NAO designs complete, our research team conducted a separate moderated validation study to test whether the new patterns actually worked in practice. The study tested four features — Set-up/Pre-flow, the To-Do List, Account Details, and Documents — across nine participants from both Pershing and non-Pershing firms.

The To-Do List: the clearest win

The To-Do List was the design decision we were most confident about going into testing. The validation confirmed it — and gave us language for why it worked that was sharper than anything we'd articulated ourselves.

"I think having a checklist off to the side, it's really nice. It shows where I'm at in the process — so many times I feel like I can get lost, especially if it's a new workflow."

— Participant 6, Ameriprise

The research team's synthesis put it plainly: the To-Do List palpably reduced cognitive load by indicating progress, location, and completion throughout the NAO process. Participants used it as both a checklist and a progress tracker simultaneously, and the ability to navigate non-linearly was called out as genuinely valuable — not a nice-to-have, but something that changed how manageable the process felt.

Users also pushed the concept further. Several participants asked whether the To-Do List could indicate who owned which tasks — surfacing the multi-participant reality of NAO directly in the interface rather than leaving it to be managed through email and phone calls.

The pre-flow: intuitive, with one friction point

Participants found the pre-flow setup logical and easy to move through. Copy existing client was immediately understood and appreciated. The suggested clients association — linking existing client profiles to the new account — was intuitive without explanation.

The friction point was account type selection. Participants didn't want a flat grid of options — they wanted types organized by how they actually think about accounts: taxable versus non-taxable, retail versus retirement. Several participants described their firm's internal mental models for account categorization and noted the current structure didn't reflect them.

"We kind of break accounts up into four different types — it's all based on taxation really. Business accounts, retirement accounts, estate accounts, and individual non-qualified accounts."

— Participant 3, Treasure Valley Financial Planning

Account Details: auto-fill as delight and concern

The inclusion of trading restrictions in Account Details — pre-populated from existing client data — was a genuine surprise. Participants called it a unique innovation and expressed enthusiasm for the time it would save. Auto-filling known beneficiaries from existing profiles was similarly well-received.

But auto-fill also introduced a new responsibility. Participants wanted a clear visual mechanism to verify that auto-filled information was accurate — a highlight, a percentage indicator for beneficiary allocations, or some signal that flagged pre-populated fields as requiring confirmation before submission.

Groups and groupings remained a persistent confusion point. Most participants said they'd more naturally assign an account to a household than a billing or performance group — a mental model mismatch that the current design hadn't resolved.

🖥️

Account Details section showing restrictions auto-filled and beneficiaries table. Auto-filled restrictions and beneficiaries were celebrated — but surfaced a new design need: how do you help users verify that pre-populated data is correct?

Documents: the select-and-send pattern landed

The Documents section received strong positive sentiment. Participants appreciated that required documents were identified automatically, and several were enthusiastic about the select-and-send model — the ability to select specific forms and send them directly from within the NAO flow rather than switching to a separate tool.

"I like the document section, the select and send... if you could go into those documents and select like the form CRS, the form CRG, and whatever prospectus you need and it just did it in there, that would be awesome."

— Participant 5, NYLife Securities

The clearest opportunity: required documents should be auto-checked and locked — visible but not removable — with optional documents surfaced in a separate section below. Participants also wanted to preview document content by clicking the hyperlink before sending, and to see suggested forms they might be missing.

🖥️

Documents section — Select and Send feature showing required and optional document categories. Documents: the select-and-send pattern resonated strongly. The next iteration would separate required from optional documents and add document preview.

KYC placement: a structural change prompted by testing

Users told us that Know Your Customer fields — broker-dealer affiliations, regulatory disclosures — felt misplaced inside Account Details. Compliance requirements have their own logic and their own responsible parties, and burying them within Account Details created cognitive friction.

The recommendation from the study was specific: KYC should sit closer to Client Details, above Account Details in the To-Do List structure. The fields themselves should also default to collapsed — most answers are "No," and the "if yes" sub-sections should only expand when a positive answer is selected.

We restructured the To-Do List order as a direct result of this finding.

Submit ≠ create: the most important language fix

The most nuanced finding from the study came at the end of the flow. The confirmation screen read "New account has been created" — but several participants pointed out that this was factually incorrect. If documents had just been sent for signature, the account hadn't been created yet. It wouldn't be created until those documents were signed, returned, and reviewed by an approver.

The design was implying a completed state that hadn't happened. And placing a "Create new account" button on the same screen as a creation confirmation made the contradiction visible.

"Yeah. Not created. It really hasn't been created until we upload documents."

— Participant 4, Davenport

Three specific fixes emerged: language that reflected the actual account status at the point of submission, removal of the confirmation/create button contradiction, and making the account number clickable so users could navigate directly to the account page once it was active.

🖥️

Confirmation screen — "New account has been created" with Submit ≠ Create annotation. Submit ≠ create: the confirmation language implied completion before completion had occurred — a meaningful trust issue in a high-stakes workflow.

Part Two
Asset Movement
💡

Discovery: A Different Problem, A Different Kind of Fear

Asset Movement looks simpler than account opening on paper. It's transactional — move money from one place to another. But the research told a different story.

Discovery for Asset Movement drew from the same Custody Client Research that anchored the NAO work, supplemented by targeted sessions focused on how advisors and operations staff actually initiated and processed transfers day to day. What emerged wasn't a complexity problem. It was a confidence problem.

The interface was creating fear

The deepest finding wasn't about missing features or slow processes. It was about trust — specifically, the lack of it.

The legacy experience offered multiple entry points into the same workflow: the Balance screen, the Profile tab, Servicing Asset Movement. Each path looked and behaved slightly differently. Forms for supporting documentation lived on a separate screen from the transfer itself, forcing reviewers to manually match documents to requests with no connective tissue between them.

The result was an interface that made advisors afraid to use it.

"I'm scared somebody's going to click 'to' and send somewhere totally different because you didn't choose what you were trying to do from the beginning."

This wasn't a minor usability issue. Advisors were moving client money. A mistake wasn't recoverable with an undo button. The design had to make the right action obvious and the wrong action nearly impossible.

Two personas, two fundamentally different needs

Research also surfaced a meaningful split — two user types with fundamentally different relationships to the same workflow.

Advisors initiated transfers occasionally. They needed guidance through the process and cared most about not making a costly mistake with client money. Speed was secondary to confidence.

Home Office and Operations staff processed transfers all day, every day. They needed to move fast, see everything at once, and get through high volumes without being walked through steps they already knew cold.

The same interface couldn't serve both equally. That tension shaped everything that came next.

Exploration: Two Users, Two Approaches

With two distinct personas pulling in different directions, we explored the solution space along two parallel tracks before narrowing to a single direction.

🖼️

Asset Movement wireframe exploration overview — showing both power user and advisor approaches side by side. Exploration phase: two tracks for two fundamentally different user needs.

The power user approach

For operations staff, we explored a single-page model where every element of the transfer — Assets, Schedule, Method, Documents — was visible simultaneously, with a Transfer Details sidebar showing a running summary on the right.

We ultimately paused development on the power user flow to concentrate on the advisor experience — it had clearer near-term priority, and the structural patterns we established there would inform the operations design in a future phase.

The advisor approach: first iteration

For advisors, our first direction used a horizontal stepper inside a modal: Accounts → Assets → Schedule → Method → Documents → Submit. Each step was a discrete page. Navigation was linear.

To pressure-test this direction, we ran a moderated usability study on V1 with 11 advisors across Cambridge, Davenport, Mutual of Omaha, Northwestern Mutual, Sensible Financial Planning, Spire Wealth Management, Wealth Enhancement Group, and Wood Opel Financial Advisors. The findings were specific and actionable.

The step-by-step structure was working — most participants said the order made sense and matched how they thought about the process. But the modal format created a clear problem: at no point could users see the full picture of what they were building. Each step replaced the last. By the time they reached Review, they were reconstructing the transfer from memory rather than reading it back from the screen.

The Documents step caused consistent confusion. Users thought it was a summary page. They expected documents to be sent to clients for signature — not uploaded by the advisor. And the single authorization path couldn't accommodate how different firms handled approvals.

"Thought Documents section was a summary page of transfer."

"Expected after submitting that the documents would be sent to client to sign."

Beyond the structural issues, the study surfaced a specific list of things V1 wasn't providing that users needed:

A transfer record they could save. Advisors aren't just completing a transaction — they're building a paper trail for client files. V1 gave them no way to do this.

"I double and triple check everything. So that summary I need is usually that summary. I save it. I save it in a PDF and put in the client's file. So I would want some type of documentation — this is what happened."

— Advisor, Best Wealth Advisory

Standing instructions at the point of need. Users expected to save and reuse common transfer configurations — destination accounts, recurring amounts, routing details — without re-entering them each time.

Critical data that was absent or unclear. Account numbers, available cash balances, tax withholding options, lot selection for security transfers, verbal authorization options, and a confirmation number upon completion. Several were missing entirely.

Clarity on recurring vs. one-time dates. Users consistently conflated the two. The interface treated them identically.

A clear next step after submission. Once submitted, the interface went quiet. Users didn't know what happened next, who was reviewing it, or when to expect confirmation.

📊

Asset Movement V1 usability study summary — key findings across 11 advisors from named firms. V1 study findings: step order worked, but visibility, documentation, standing instructions, and post-submission clarity all needed to change.

The guidance was working. The visibility, the documentation, and the flexibility weren't.

🔧

The Design: The Collapsing Stepper

The V1 feedback pointed to a clear tension: advisors needed the guidance of a stepped flow, but they also needed to see the full transfer take shape as they built it. Those two things felt like opposites — until we found a structure that could hold both.

How it works

Instead of separate pages, each step lives inline on the same screen. As users complete a step, it collapses into a compact summary card showing just the essential information — with an Edit button to reopen it at any time. The next step expands automatically below.

By the time users reach Review, they haven't navigated away from anything. The entire transfer — accounts, assets, schedule, method, documents — is visible on one screen as a series of confirmed cards. There's nothing to reconstruct from memory.

Direction locked from the start

The feature that addressed the fear finding most directly: once accounts are confirmed in step one, the direction of the transfer — "Hayden Oswin Individual → JP Morgan Chase" — is locked and visible at the top of the screen for the rest of the flow.

No matter how many steps follow, the answer to "where is this money going?" is always on screen. The anxiety that the legacy interface created by obscuring direction until the final review simply doesn't exist in this model.

Saved instructions surfaced at the point of need

Research showed that advisors frequently repeated the same transfers — the same accounts, the same destinations, the same amounts. The legacy experience required manually re-entering everything each time.

The redesign surfaces Saved Instructions and Recently Used transfers directly in the destination selection step — not buried in settings, not requiring a separate lookup. Common transfers are one tap away at the exact moment they're needed.

Method follows asset type

One key structural decision came from the design lead: instead of asking users to choose a transfer method upfront, we ask what assets are being transferred first. Available methods then filter automatically based on what makes sense for that asset type.

This removes a failure mode — users selecting incompatible method and asset combinations — and removes cognitive load from advisors who may not know which methods apply to their specific situation.

Edit without losing context

Every completed summary card carries an Edit button. Users can return to any earlier step, make a change, and the subsequent steps update to reflect it — without starting over or losing work already done.

This was a direct response to the anxiety the legacy interface created. In the old experience, going back often meant losing progress. In the collapsing stepper, nothing is permanent until submission, and everything is recoverable.

🖥️

Collapsing stepper with one step reopened for editing — surrounding summary cards still visible. Edit at any point: change what you need, keep everything else. No starting over.

The Common Thread

Two workflows. Different timelines. Different risk profiles. Different users. NAO is a multi-participant process that can take weeks. Asset Movement is a high-stakes transaction that takes minutes. On the surface they have almost nothing in common.

But the same five principles emerged from both — independently, through separate research, separate exploration phases, and separate validation efforts.

Make complexity visible, not smaller

The instinct in both workflows was to simplify — to reduce, hide, or abstract complexity so users wouldn't feel overwhelmed. The research said the opposite. Users weren't afraid of complexity. They were afraid of hidden complexity. They needed to see the full shape of what they were doing.

In NAO, that meant showing every section of the workflow upfront in the To-Do List rather than revealing it one step at a time. In Asset Movement, it meant keeping every completed step visible on screen as a summary card rather than replacing it with the next one.

The design principle: don't hide what's hard. Make it navigable.

Structure that flexes

Both workflows had users who needed to move non-linearly. In NAO, advisors rarely have complete client information when they start — they need to jump between sections as information arrives. In Asset Movement, experienced users needed to edit a step they'd already completed without starting over.

Linear forms punish non-linear work. The design in both cases offered guidance without enforcing sequence — a structure that held the user's place while giving them freedom to move.

Progress as a first-class feature

In both workflows, users had no reliable way to know where they were, how much remained, or what was blocking them. This wasn't a minor friction — it was the source of anxiety that made advisors double-check everything, call their reps, and avoid the tool entirely.

Progress indicators aren't decorative. In NAO, completion states in the To-Do List told users what was done and what wasn't. In Asset Movement, the locked direction header and building summary cards told users exactly where their transfer stood at every moment. Both designs treated progress visibility as functional, not ornamental.

Information at the point of need

Users shouldn't have to go looking for what they need mid-task. In NAO, the send-to-client toggle lives on the form section where the need arises — not in settings, not in a separate flow. In Asset Movement, saved instructions and recently used transfers surface at the destination selection step — the exact moment they're useful.

The pattern: anticipate where users will need something and surface it there, not somewhere logical but elsewhere.

Honest end states

Both workflows had interfaces that implied completion before it had occurred. In NAO, "New account has been created" appeared before documents were signed or approvals granted. In Asset Movement, submission implied resolution when review still followed.

In both cases, the fix was the same: make the interface reflect what was actually true at that moment. Not aspirational. Not simplified. Honest.

Direction locked from step one Transfer destination confirmed and visible throughout
Saved instructions surfaced at point of need Common transfers accessible in one tap at the right moment
Method filtered by asset type Invalid method/asset combinations eliminated by design
Confirmation state and next steps clarified No ambiguity about what happens after submission

What remained open

Both designs are validated and staged for implementation as BNY Pershing's platform consolidation continues.

For NAO, the validation pointed toward a clear next iteration — multi-participant task ownership in the To-Do List, document preview before sending, and clearer account type categorization by tax treatment. For Asset Movement, the V1 study surfaced design questions that carry into the next phase: how verbal authorization options get surfaced, how recurring and one-time transfer dates are distinguished visually, and how confirmation numbers are surfaced immediately upon submission.

These aren't design failures. They're the natural output of a research loop that closed well: you learn what to do next because you tested what you built.

🔮

Reflection

The most striking thing about this project wasn't the design. It was the research gap.

The pain points advisors described in 2024 — the fragmented tools, the hidden complexity, the inability to track progress, the fear of making an irreversible mistake — matched documented research from 2018 almost exactly. Six years. The same problems, described in nearly the same language, by different people at different firms.

That's not a coincidence. It's what happens when structural problems get addressed with incremental solutions. Forms get shorter. Steps get labeled. Tooltips get added. But the underlying architecture stays the same. And so the pain stays the same.

What this project required wasn't a better form. It was a different mental model of what the workflow was doing. NAO isn't a form to fill out — it's a project to manage. Asset Movement isn't a step-by-step process — it's a high-stakes decision that needs to be visible from start to confirmation. The design followed from the reframe, not the other way around.

Working on a platform in transition also shaped how I thought about every decision. These designs don't live in a finished system. They live in a future state that's still being built, feature by feature, workflow by workflow. That changes the design constraint: you're not solving for today's edge cases. You're establishing patterns that need to hold up as the system grows around them. The To-Do List, the collapsing stepper, the send-to-client model — these aren't one-off solutions. They're structural decisions that will carry forward into every workflow that follows.

The most durable design decisions in this project weren't the ones that solved for the immediate problem. They were the ones that would still make sense when the problem evolved.

Turning Overlooked Revenue into Action

Fully Paid Lending lets advisors earn fees by lending their clients' shares—but the tool designed to surface these opportunities was collecting dust. I led the redesign of an underutilized NetX360 feature into something advisors would actually use.

Dimension Details
My Role Lead Studio Designer
Team Design Lead, Development Lead, 2 Developers, Product SME, Project Lead
Timeline 1 year (kickoff to client release)
Outcome Shipped February 2026
⚡

The Tool No One Used

Fully Paid Lending is a straightforward value proposition: advisors can lend shares their clients already own and generate passive revenue. BNY Pershing had a tool to help advisors identify these opportunities—but adoption was dismal.

The existing Opportunity View page in NetX360 had all the data. It showed potential revenue, participating clients, and historical performance. On paper, it should have been valuable. In practice, advisors were ignoring it entirely.

"The tool had the data. It just made users work too hard to extract value from it."

🔍

Listening to the Workarounds

Without pre-existing research on the FPL experience, I partnered with our research team to conduct moderated interviews with six participants across five firms—prime brokerage, operations, relationship management, and securities lending roles at BTIG, Citi Wealth, RL Lafferty, Pershing, Vantax, and Cetera.

The research objective was specific: understand how users currently interact with Opportunity View in NetX360, identify which features are meeting their needs, and uncover where the tool was falling short. What we found wasn't a list of missing features. It was a picture of a tool users had learned to work around—and the specific points where it was losing them.

Bubbles Were Decoration

The interface's centerpiece was a bubble visualization—larger bubbles indicated bigger opportunities. The assumption built into the design was that visual hierarchy would help advisors prioritize at a glance.

The reality was more complicated. Users understood what the bubbles represented, but most found drilling into individual bubbles inefficient. Identifying which account position corresponded to which opportunity required clicking through separate views with no persistent context—by the time users reached the position level, they'd lost sight of the opportunity that sent them there.

Most participants resolved this by exporting everything to Excel, where they could sort, filter, and cross-reference with Bloomberg at their own pace. Some skipped the bubble visualization entirely and went straight to the export. The Historical View—a feature designed to show trend data over time—was largely ignored. Most users weren't aware of its value or hadn't found a workflow that incorporated it.

"Some users do not engage with the bubbles at all and rely on Excel exports."

The Numbers Raised More Questions Than Answers

Even when users engaged with the data, they couldn't fully trust what they were seeing. The interface showed estimated revenue potential but didn't explain how those figures were calculated. Several participants suspected the tool was assuming static lending rates—figures that didn't reflect daily market changes. Others believed the revenue estimates represented maximum possible conditions rather than realistic projections, which meant any number the tool surfaced needed to be verified before it could inform a client conversation.

Users needed additional context to assess opportunity validity: security utilization, trading frequency, short interest data, real-time loan rates. None of this was available in the interface.

The most telling workaround: some participants were reaching out to internal contacts at BNY Pershing to validate opportunities before acting on them. The tool existed precisely to remove that step. Instead, it had made it necessary.

"Some feel that Opportunity View assumes static rates, which may not reflect daily market changes."

📈

Data points above the fold in the legacy interface—revenue tracking and participant data were present, but no methodology context explained how figures were calculated or what assumptions they reflected.

Friction Killed Enrollment

Both problems above assumed a user who was willing to act. But the third finding revealed that even advisors who identified a credible opportunity had nowhere to go with it.

Onboarding a client into the lending program required manually emailing documents, collecting signatures outside the platform, and uploading completed agreements back in. Each step introduced delay and the possibility of something getting lost. The tool could show you the opportunity. It couldn't help you capture it.

Users who did pursue enrollment described the process as operationally burdensome enough to make them reconsider whether the opportunity was worth it—especially for smaller positions where the revenue upside didn't justify the overhead.

"The process for onboarding clients into the lending program is inefficient. Users must manually email and upload documents, creating unnecessary friction."

What the Research Pointed Toward

Taken together, the findings described a tool that was failing users at three distinct moments: when they tried to scan for opportunities, when they tried to evaluate them, and when they tried to act on them. The problems weren't isolated—they compounded. A tool that required Excel exports to be usable, couldn't explain its own numbers, and dead-ended at enrollment wasn't going to get adopted no matter how good the underlying data was.

The research conclusion was direct: enhancements focused on improved data accuracy, streamlined processes, and better usability would significantly increase adoption and effectiveness. That became the brief.

🎯

Reframing the Problem

The research painted a clear picture: this wasn't a feature problem. It was a usability problem compounded by a trust problem.

The original interface asked users to interact with data in ways that didn't match their mental models. It prioritized visualization over utility. It obscured methodology instead of revealing it. And it dead-ended at the moment of action.

The core design challenge became:

The Core Question

"How do you surface dense financial data in a way that supports quick scanning and deep investigation—without forcing users into a separate tool?"

✨

From Visualization to Information

The most significant design decision was killing the bubble visualization entirely.

Research showed users were bypassing it for Excel exports. They wanted to scan across offices and advisors quickly, drill into specific positions when needed, and take action without leaving context. Bubbles didn't support any of these behaviors.

I explored multiple directions—bar charts, various table configurations, hybrid approaches—before landing on an expandable nested table as the primary interface.

The Nested Table

The final design uses a three-level hierarchy: Office → Advisor → Investor. Users can scan across the top level to identify high-opportunity offices, expand to see which advisors are driving those numbers, and drill further to individual investor positions.

This pattern solved several problems simultaneously:

  • Scanning: Managers can quickly compare performance across their network without clicking into separate views
  • Context preservation: Expanding a row keeps surrounding data visible, unlike the old drill-down flow that lost context with each click
  • Flexibility: The same structure works for both manager and advisor views, reducing cognitive load when switching between perspectives

Reclaiming the Space Above the Fold

A consistent complaint across BNY Pershing products was content being pushed below the scroll. The legacy FPL page stacked filters, tabs, summary statistics, and navigation elements—leaving actual data buried.

I audited every element above the fold and challenged whether it earned its placement.

What Got Removed

The IBD/Office dropdown filters. These duplicated functionality now handled by the table's expandable hierarchy and inline filtering.

The separate "Top Opportunities" section. Valuable content, but it competed with the primary table. I moved this to a dedicated "View by Ticker" tab where users seeking specific securities could find them intentionally.

The Participating/Non-Participating tab split. Instead of forcing users to toggle between views, I added Enrolled and Not Enrolled columns inline. One view, complete picture.

What Got Added

Sparklines for performance trends. A small inline visualization showing YTD performance lets users spot patterns without leaving the table. Clicking opens a modal with the full chart—progressive disclosure that keeps the primary view scannable.

A "Learn and Invite" tab. Consolidating program information and the enrollment flow in one place addressed the friction that killed adoption. Advisors can understand the program and take action without leaving the tool.

From Insight to Action

Identifying opportunities is only valuable if users can act on them. The legacy enrollment process required manual emails and document uploads—friction that meant opportunities sat untouched.

The invite worksheet lets users select eligible accounts directly from the table and send enrollment forms in a single flow. Status is visible inline (Enrolled, Not Enrolled, partial enrollment states), and users can preview the actual agreement before sending.

🎉

The Outcome

The redesigned Fully Paid Lending experience shipped to all Introducing Broker-Dealers in February 2026—a full end-to-end rollout of a digitally enabled lending experience built to help IBDs unlock new revenue streams and deliver differentiated value to their advisors and investors.

The launch marked the completion of a feature that had gone from an underutilized data page to a commercial priority. Client Executive teams across BNY Pershing were briefed to position FPL as a strategic revenue lever—for both the IBD and their end investors.

What Shipped

The final experience delivered three integrated capabilities:

Opportunity Identification — full visibility into eligible investor holdings, estimated incremental revenue potential, enhanced security-level search across accounts, and the ability to upload off-platform positions for assessment.

Digital E-Signature & Consent Workflow — advisor-initiated digital enrollment with individual and bulk invitation capability, a one-click enrollment experience, and a full audit trail to support compliance and reduce operational friction.

Participant & Revenue Visibility — real-time view of active FPL participants and revenue generated at the position level.

Proof of Concept: $49,900 in One Day

The most direct validation came from the March 2026 beta, which ran with five IBDs who had participated in designing and testing the application. One transaction during that beta captured exactly what the redesign was built to enable.

A product partner from the Collateral Funding and Trading team flagged a significant rate spike on a security held by one of the beta IBD's clients—the rate had moved from -2% to between -50% and -99%. The IBD reached out to the client, who had previously been hesitant to enroll at the lower rate. The client signed, uploaded the agreement, and it was processed the same day.

The client then moved additional shares of the same security to Pershing, increasing the lendable position beyond what had originally been identified in the Opportunity View. By the following morning, the entire position—$27 million—had been lent at an average rate of -91%.

Beta Result

The client earned $49,900 in a single day. The IBD earned $10,700. Pershing earned $10,700.

"I am very happy to report that we were able to monetize on an opportunity that was identified in the new Opportunity View. This is a great story about the value of this tool."

— Product Partner, Collateral Funding and Trading Team

Why This Maps Back to the Design

This outcome didn't happen because the data existed. The data had always existed. It happened because the redesign made that data actionable at the right moment.

The rate spike was surfaced through the opportunity identification layer—the advisor would not have caught a move of that magnitude without the tool flagging it. The enrollment happened quickly because the friction that previously killed adoption had been replaced by a single integrated flow. And the client moving more assets to Pershing was a downstream consequence of a positive, low-friction experience—an outcome that couldn't have been designed for directly, but that a well-designed tool made possible.

The beta context matters here. Five IBDs co-designed and tested this application before launch. This wasn't a tool shipped into the wild and hoped for the best—it was shaped by the people who would use it, which is why it was ready to support a real transaction from day one of the broader rollout.

This project reinforced something I keep relearning: "underutilized" is usually a design problem wearing a feature costume.

That's what transparency of methodology and real-time visibility actually buys you—not just usability, but confidence in the numbers. And confidence is what turns a passive data page into a revenue instrument.

The five IBDs who co-designed this application didn't just help us build a better tool. They made sure the tool was trusted before it was launched. That trust is what made the first real transaction possible.

Building the Platform I Wished Existed

Every college student faces the same problem: finding off-campus housing is chaos. I didn't just design a solution—I founded a company to build it. Purple Panda Rentals is the rental platform I wished existed when I was a student.

Dimension Details
My Role Founder & Product Designer
Timeline 2 months (mobile app design)
Research 4 interviews, 270 survey respondents
Outcome Validated mobile app design for launched platform
😤

The Problem Was Personal

Finding off-campus housing as a college student is a mess. You're competing with other students for limited listings, negotiating with landlords who don't take you seriously, and trying to coordinate move-in dates around academic calendars that don't align with standard lease terms.

I lived this. So did everyone I knew. The options were Zillow (not built for students), Craigslist (sketchy), or word-of-mouth (limited). None of them understood the specific needs of college renters.

So I started Purple Panda Rentals—a rental listing platform built specifically for college students. The website launched in December 2023. The next step was mobile.

"Along with saving money, students benefit from the independence and housing flexibility that off-campus housing provides."

🔍

Validating the Problem at Scale

Before designing the mobile app, I needed to confirm my assumptions weren't just my own experience. I ran a nationwide survey with 270 college students and conducted interviews with four former students about their leasing and searching habits.

What I Expected

The survey confirmed what I suspected:

  • 76% currently live off-campus
  • 53% use Zillow to find housing (a tool not built for them)
  • 57% said proximity to campus was the most important factor
  • 51% said rental price was the deciding factor

Students were using generic tools because nothing better existed.

What Surprised Me

The survey also revealed pain points I hadn't fully appreciated:

  • 81% prefer to stay in one apartment for multiple semesters—stability matters more than I assumed
  • 42% found it frustrating to find someone to sublease their apartment between semesters
  • 34% had maintenance and landlord issues with no way to vet reputation beforehand
  • 15% struggled to calculate total cost when rent, utilities, and living expenses were listed separately

The sublease problem was bigger than I expected. Students weren't just looking for housing—they needed to offload housing when plans changed.

"Finding a student to sublease their apartment was frustrating between semesters."

🎯

The Competitive Gap

I audited both direct competitors (student-focused platforms like College Pads) and indirect competitors (Zillow, Apartments.com). The pattern was clear:

Mainstream platforms had the best user experience and largest inventory—but no student-specific features.

Student platforms had relevant features—but clunky interfaces and limited listings.

No one had married Zillow's usability with features tailored to the college experience.

✨

Designing the Mobile Experience

With the research foundation set, I moved into designing the mobile app. The website was live; now students needed to search on the go.

The Sublease Decision

When defining the app's scope, I faced a tradeoff: include a sublease section or a roommate matcher? Both had value. I couldn't do both well in the initial release.

The research made the call. 42% of students found subleasing frustrating—a concrete pain point with high frequency. Roommate matching got positive feedback in interviews, but sublease functionality addressed a more urgent, more frequent need.

I prioritized sublease. Roommate matching went on the roadmap for future releases.

"Based on the students I researched, a sublease section prioritized over finding a roommate. This finding surprised me."

The UI System

Purple Panda's brand was already established from the website—bright, approachable, student-friendly. The mobile app needed to extend that identity while meeting iOS conventions.

🧪

Testing with Real Users

I built three prototypes in Figma and ran usability tests through Maze with 17 participants. Three tasks, representing the core use cases:

  1. Create an account and complete onboarding
  2. Search with filters and favorite a listing
  3. Find a sublease and message the student

What Worked

Task 1: Account creation — 100% success rate. Onboarding questions were appropriate and completion time was adequate.

What Didn't

Task 2: Search and favorite — 85% success rate. Users struggled with the filter screen (clicking text instead of toggles) and couldn't easily see the heart icon for favoriting.

Task 3: Sublease and messaging — 63% success rate. Users couldn't find their way back to the inbox after sending a message. The back arrow was too subtle.

The problems were specific and fixable. I iterated.

📈

After Iteration

I ran the tests again with the updated designs.

Task Before After
Search and favorite 85% 100%
Sublease and messaging 63% 100%

The changes worked. But testing also surfaced a deeper issue I hadn't fully solved: trust.

Users questioned whether they could trust a stranger subletting an apartment. Security and reputation weren't addressed in the current design. For future iterations, I noted the need for verified student profiles or review systems—features that would take more than UI changes to implement properly.

🔮

Reflection

Purple Panda taught me the difference between designing for a prompt and designing for a real problem.

When you're the founder, there's no brief to fall back on. You have to define the problem yourself, validate it with real people, and make tradeoffs that affect a real business. The sublease vs. roommate decision wasn't academic—it determined what the product would actually do.

The usability testing reinforced something I keep relearning: you can't predict where users will struggle. I thought the filter screen was straightforward. Users disagreed. The only way to find out is to put the design in front of real people and watch what happens.

What's next: the publisher side of the platform (for landlords listing properties) and the trust features that testing revealed were missing. Building a product is never done—you just ship what you can and keep learning.

Designing My Own Agentic Interface

A personal agentic system that actually knew me. Not a chatbot. Not another productivity app. A framework I owned, running on my own hardware, that could get smarter about my goals over time.

DimensionDetails
RoleDesigner & Builder
ToolsClaude, Claude Code, SwiftUI, OpenCLAW
Timeline2 weeks (nights & weekends)
StatusShipped & In Active Use

When OpenCLAW launched in early 2026, I saw it as the missing piece for something I'd been wanting to build: a personal agentic system that actually knew me. Not a chatbot. Not another productivity app. A framework I owned, running on my own hardware, that could get smarter about my goals over time. I built the framework in two weeks alongside my full-time role at BNY. Then I built a native iOS app to live inside it — because the interface I was using wasn't good enough for what the system had become.

🧩

Nothing Knew Me

I wasn't drowning in tools. I was drowning in context-switching between them.

Design questions in one Claude project. Work strategy in another. Writing drafts scattered across Notion and half-finished conversations. A firehose of AI and design content with no way to filter what actually mattered to my trajectory. Every conversation started from zero. I repeated context constantly. Nothing compounded.

The deeper frustration was control. I wanted a system that lived on my own devices, updated over time, and gave me a coherent picture of what mattered — without syncing my thinking to someone else's servers.

OpenCLAW gave me the architecture to build that.

⚙️

Four Agents, One System

Before writing any code, I sat down with Claude and designed the agent structure. The core decision was to separate concerns from the start — four specialized agents rather than one assistant trying to do everything.

Penn — Router
Routes everything. Every message gets triaged by Penn, which determines which agent should handle it and routes accordingly. Lightweight by design — Penn doesn't do much besides connect the system.
BNY Strategist — Work Context
Owns my work context. Builds and maintains identity cards for the people I work with — updated each time I mention someone or drop in a meeting note. Keeps a running picture of key relationships and projects that I'd otherwise be rebuilding from memory.
Design Thinker — Writing Partner
Holds drafts, ideas, and thinking patterns. Connected to the Design Thinker Substack workflow — I can read an article through the Learning Assistant, spark an idea, and route directly to Design Thinker to develop it.
Learning Assistant — Firehose Filter
Pulls from 30+ RSS feeds across AI, design, and startups. Reads everything, surfaces what's relevant to my specific trajectory, and generates digests with TL;DRs. The content finds me, filtered to what matters.

The whole system runs on my Windows PC via a gateway and REST API, with Tailscale for secure remote access from anywhere.

📱

Why a Native App: Chat Isn't Enough

Telegram worked — eventually. Getting Penn to reliably route messages to the right agent took iteration, but it got there. The problem wasn't that Telegram broke. It was that chat is the wrong interface for living inside a system.

Everything landed in a single thread. When cron jobs fired — weekly digests, Monday briefs, content pipeline outputs — the results scrolled past and disappeared. I couldn't see my work context alongside my learning queue alongside Penn's weekly synthesis without prompting each one separately. There was no spatial view. No push notifications when something time-sensitive surfaced.

I needed something I carried with me that felt less like a conversation and more like a dashboard — one that surfaced what mattered without me having to ask.

So I built Navori.

✨

Key Decisions

The priority was connection, not visual polish.

The hardest part of this project wasn't the UI — it was getting the iOS app to reliably communicate with the OpenCLAW gateway running on my PC. The connection follows a specific handshake protocol: connect → challenge/nonce → hello-ok → then chat with the appropriate agentId. Getting that handshake right, and building fallback logic that tries local WiFi first then drops to Tailscale, was the real design and engineering challenge. Everything visual came second.

Four tabs reflecting four agent domains.

Today, Work, Write, Learn. Each tab surfaces the output of its corresponding agent in a structured view rather than a chat thread — projects and relationships from BNY Strategist, draft ideas from Design Thinker, curated articles from Learning Assistant, and Penn's daily synthesis at the top of Today.

Push notifications for cron job outputs.

The Monday Brief and weekly Learning Digest were the primary reason I needed to move beyond Telegram. Scheduled outputs that arrive while I'm working needed to interrupt me — not disappear into a scroll. Notifications made the system feel alive rather than passive.

The weekly check-in as a deliberate ritual.

Every Friday, a card surfaces a short reflection prompt. It's a small interaction with a real behavioral intent: feeding structured reflection back into the agents so the system compounds rather than stays static.

Connection transparency.

A small indicator shows whether the app is talking to the gateway over local WiFi or Tailscale. A minor detail, but it mattered for my own trust in the system — I always knew its state.

🪞

Reflection

The most important decision in this project wasn't a UI choice. It was the architecture decision to separate agent concerns from the start. Each agent owning its own memory and domain meant the system could grow without becoming a monolith. Penn staying lightweight as a router rather than a heavy orchestrator meant it stayed fast. These are product decisions as much as technical ones — and getting them right at the beginning is what made the app genuinely useful rather than just technically interesting.

"Agentic interfaces require a fundamentally different design vocabulary than conversational ones. Chat assumes you'll ask. Agents can surface."

The design job shifts from "how do I make this interaction clear?" to "what does the user need to see before they even know to ask?" That's a different problem — and one that's going to matter increasingly as AI moves from assistants to systems that act on your behalf.

I'm still building. The work agent has real limitations without integration into my work machine. Several features — piping cron job outputs directly into the app feed, in-app insight tracking after the weekly digest — are on the roadmap. But the system is useful today, which is the only bar that matters.

That instinct — ship something real, learn from using it, keep building — is what I want to bring into my next role.
