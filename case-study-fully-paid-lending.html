Turning Overlooked Revenue into Action

Fully Paid Lending lets advisors earn fees by lending their clients' shares—but the tool designed to surface these opportunities was collecting dust. I led the redesign of an underutilized NetX360 feature into something advisors would actually use.

Dimension Details
My Role Lead Studio Designer
Team Design Lead, Development Lead, 2 Developers, Product SME, Project Lead
Timeline 1 year (kickoff to client release)
Outcome Shipped February 2026
⚡

The Tool No One Used

Fully Paid Lending is a straightforward value proposition: advisors can lend shares their clients already own and generate passive revenue. BNY Pershing had a tool to help advisors identify these opportunities—but adoption was dismal.

The existing Opportunity View page in NetX360 had all the data. It showed potential revenue, participating clients, and historical performance. On paper, it should have been valuable. In practice, advisors were ignoring it entirely.

"The tool had the data. It just made users work too hard to extract value from it."

🔍

Listening to the Workarounds

Without pre-existing research on the FPL experience, I partnered with our research team to conduct moderated interviews with six participants across five firms—prime brokerage, operations, relationship management, and securities lending roles at BTIG, Citi Wealth, RL Lafferty, Pershing, Vantax, and Cetera.

The research objective was specific: understand how users currently interact with Opportunity View in NetX360, identify which features are meeting their needs, and uncover where the tool was falling short. What we found wasn't a list of missing features. It was a picture of a tool users had learned to work around—and the specific points where it was losing them.

Bubbles Were Decoration

The interface's centerpiece was a bubble visualization—larger bubbles indicated bigger opportunities. The assumption built into the design was that visual hierarchy would help advisors prioritize at a glance.

The reality was more complicated. Users understood what the bubbles represented, but most found drilling into individual bubbles inefficient. Identifying which account position corresponded to which opportunity required clicking through separate views with no persistent context—by the time users reached the position level, they'd lost sight of the opportunity that sent them there.

Most participants resolved this by exporting everything to Excel, where they could sort, filter, and cross-reference with Bloomberg at their own pace. Some skipped the bubble visualization entirely and went straight to the export. The Historical View—a feature designed to show trend data over time—was largely ignored. Most users weren't aware of its value or hadn't found a workflow that incorporated it.

"Some users do not engage with the bubbles at all and rely on Excel exports."

The Numbers Raised More Questions Than Answers

Even when users engaged with the data, they couldn't fully trust what they were seeing. The interface showed estimated revenue potential but didn't explain how those figures were calculated. Several participants suspected the tool was assuming static lending rates—figures that didn't reflect daily market changes. Others believed the revenue estimates represented maximum possible conditions rather than realistic projections, which meant any number the tool surfaced needed to be verified before it could inform a client conversation.

Users needed additional context to assess opportunity validity: security utilization, trading frequency, short interest data, real-time loan rates. None of this was available in the interface.

The most telling workaround: some participants were reaching out to internal contacts at BNY Pershing to validate opportunities before acting on them. The tool existed precisely to remove that step. Instead, it had made it necessary.

"Some feel that Opportunity View assumes static rates, which may not reflect daily market changes."

📈

Data points above the fold in the legacy interface—revenue tracking and participant data were present, but no methodology context explained how figures were calculated or what assumptions they reflected.

Friction Killed Enrollment

Both problems above assumed a user who was willing to act. But the third finding revealed that even advisors who identified a credible opportunity had nowhere to go with it.

Onboarding a client into the lending program required manually emailing documents, collecting signatures outside the platform, and uploading completed agreements back in. Each step introduced delay and the possibility of something getting lost. The tool could show you the opportunity. It couldn't help you capture it.

Users who did pursue enrollment described the process as operationally burdensome enough to make them reconsider whether the opportunity was worth it—especially for smaller positions where the revenue upside didn't justify the overhead.

"The process for onboarding clients into the lending program is inefficient. Users must manually email and upload documents, creating unnecessary friction."

What the Research Pointed Toward

Taken together, the findings described a tool that was failing users at three distinct moments: when they tried to scan for opportunities, when they tried to evaluate them, and when they tried to act on them. The problems weren't isolated—they compounded. A tool that required Excel exports to be usable, couldn't explain its own numbers, and dead-ended at enrollment wasn't going to get adopted no matter how good the underlying data was.

The research conclusion was direct: enhancements focused on improved data accuracy, streamlined processes, and better usability would significantly increase adoption and effectiveness. That became the brief.

🎯

Reframing the Problem

The research painted a clear picture: this wasn't a feature problem. It was a usability problem compounded by a trust problem.

The original interface asked users to interact with data in ways that didn't match their mental models. It prioritized visualization over utility. It obscured methodology instead of revealing it. And it dead-ended at the moment of action.

The core design challenge became:

The Core Question

"How do you surface dense financial data in a way that supports quick scanning and deep investigation—without forcing users into a separate tool?"

✨

From Visualization to Information

The most significant design decision was killing the bubble visualization entirely.

Research showed users were bypassing it for Excel exports. They wanted to scan across offices and advisors quickly, drill into specific positions when needed, and take action without leaving context. Bubbles didn't support any of these behaviors.

I explored multiple directions—bar charts, various table configurations, hybrid approaches—before landing on an expandable nested table as the primary interface.

The Nested Table

The final design uses a three-level hierarchy: Office → Advisor → Investor. Users can scan across the top level to identify high-opportunity offices, expand to see which advisors are driving those numbers, and drill further to individual investor positions.

This pattern solved several problems simultaneously:

  • Scanning: Managers can quickly compare performance across their network without clicking into separate views
  • Context preservation: Expanding a row keeps surrounding data visible, unlike the old drill-down flow that lost context with each click
  • Flexibility: The same structure works for both manager and advisor views, reducing cognitive load when switching between perspectives

Reclaiming the Space Above the Fold

A consistent complaint across BNY Pershing products was content being pushed below the scroll. The legacy FPL page stacked filters, tabs, summary statistics, and navigation elements—leaving actual data buried.

I audited every element above the fold and challenged whether it earned its placement.

What Got Removed

The IBD/Office dropdown filters. These duplicated functionality now handled by the table's expandable hierarchy and inline filtering.

The separate "Top Opportunities" section. Valuable content, but it competed with the primary table. Moved to a dedicated "View by Ticker" tab where users seeking specific securities could find them intentionally.

The Participating/Non-Participating tab split. Instead of forcing users to toggle between views, Enrolled and Not Enrolled columns are now inline. One view, complete picture.

What Got Added

Sparklines for performance trends. A small inline visualization showing YTD performance lets users spot patterns without leaving the table. Clicking opens a modal with the full chart—progressive disclosure that keeps the primary view scannable.

A "Learn and Invite" tab. Consolidating program information and the enrollment flow in one place addressed the friction that killed adoption. Advisors can understand the program and take action without leaving the tool.

From Insight to Action

Identifying opportunities is only valuable if users can act on them. The legacy enrollment process required manual emails and document uploads—friction that meant opportunities sat untouched.

The invite worksheet lets users select eligible accounts directly from the table and send enrollment forms in a single flow. Status is visible inline (Enrolled, Not Enrolled, partial enrollment states), and users can preview the actual agreement before sending.

🎉

The Outcome

The redesigned Fully Paid Lending experience shipped to all Introducing Broker-Dealers in February 2026—a full end-to-end rollout of a digitally enabled lending experience built to help IBDs unlock new revenue streams and deliver differentiated value to their advisors and investors.

The launch marked the completion of a feature that had gone from an underutilized data page to a commercial priority. Client Executive teams across BNY Pershing were briefed to position FPL as a strategic revenue lever—for both the IBD and their end investors.

What Shipped

The final experience delivered three integrated capabilities:

Opportunity Identification — full visibility into eligible investor holdings, estimated incremental revenue potential, enhanced security-level search across accounts, and the ability to upload off-platform positions for assessment.

Digital E-Signature & Consent Workflow — advisor-initiated digital enrollment with individual and bulk invitation capability, a one-click enrollment experience, and a full audit trail to support compliance and reduce operational friction.

Participant & Revenue Visibility — real-time view of active FPL participants and revenue generated at the position level.

Proof of Concept: $49,900 in One Day

The most direct validation came from the March 2026 beta, which ran with five IBDs who had participated in designing and testing the application. One transaction during that beta captured exactly what the redesign was built to enable.

A product partner from the Collateral Funding and Trading team flagged a significant rate spike on a security held by one of the beta IBD's clients—the rate had moved from -2% to between -50% and -99%. The IBD reached out to the client, who had previously been hesitant to enroll at the lower rate. The client signed, uploaded the agreement, and it was processed the same day.

The client then moved additional shares of the same security to Pershing, increasing the lendable position beyond what had originally been identified in the Opportunity View. By the following morning, the entire position—$27 million—had been lent at an average rate of -91%.

Beta Result

The client earned $49,900 in a single day. The IBD earned $10,700. Pershing earned $10,700.

"I am very happy to report that we were able to monetize on an opportunity that was identified in the new Opportunity View. This is a great story about the value of this tool."

— Product Partner, Collateral Funding and Trading Team

Why This Maps Back to the Design

This outcome didn't happen because the data existed. The data had always existed. It happened because the redesign made that data actionable at the right moment.

The rate spike was surfaced through the opportunity identification layer—the advisor would not have caught a move of that magnitude without the tool flagging it. The enrollment happened quickly because the friction that previously killed adoption had been replaced by a single integrated flow. And the client moving more assets to Pershing was a downstream consequence of a positive, low-friction experience—an outcome that couldn't have been designed for directly, but that a well-designed tool made possible.

The beta context matters here. Five IBDs co-designed and tested this application before launch. This wasn't a tool shipped into the wild and hoped for the best—it was shaped by the people who would use it, which is why it was ready to support a real transaction from day one of the broader rollout.

Reflection

This project reinforced something I keep relearning: "underutilized" is usually a design problem wearing a feature costume.

The original FPL page had the data. It even had the right idea—help advisors find revenue opportunities. But it presented information in ways that didn't match how users actually worked. They needed tables, not bubbles. They needed inline context, not drill-down journeys. They needed to act, not just observe.

The biggest lever wasn't adding features. It was removing friction and consolidating information so the tool could get out of the way of the work.

What the beta outcome confirmed was something harder to design for directly: trust. The client who had been hesitant at -2% signed at -91% not because the rate changed—it was always going to change. It happened because the advisor had a tool that surfaced the opportunity credibly and in time to act. That's what transparency of methodology and real-time visibility actually buys you—not just usability, but confidence in the numbers. And confidence is what turns a passive data page into a revenue instrument.

The five IBDs who co-designed this application didn't just help us build a better tool. They made sure the tool was trusted before it was launched. That trust is what made the first real transaction possible.
